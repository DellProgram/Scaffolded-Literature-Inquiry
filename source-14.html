Part IV · Who Gets to Define a Legacy?

Source 14 of 14 · Final Source

“If There Is No Struggle, There Is No Progress”

More than a century after Frederick Douglass’s death, artist Amos Paul Kennedy Jr. used one of Douglass’s best-known statements in a modern letterpress print. Your final source asks you to consider how historical words are reused, redesigned, and given new meaning for later audiences.

Source Type
Modern letterpress poster / visual source
Date
ca. 2022
Artist
Amos Paul Kennedy Jr.
Classification
Primary source for modern memory of Douglass

Source Context

A nineteenth-century voice is speaking to a twenty-first-century audience.

This print was created long after Douglass’s lifetime, so it cannot tell us directly how people in the nineteenth century responded to him. Instead, it is evidence of how Douglass’s words continue to be selected and presented in the present.

How to Analyze It

Design

Study scale, typography, repetition, and arrangement.

Context

Remember that the quotation has been separated from its original speech.

Legacy

Decide what the modern poster reveals about how Douglass is remembered.

Guiding Questions

Consider how Douglass’s words live beyond his lifetime.

Use the poster itself as evidence, but also bring forward what you know from the earlier primary sources.

  1. 1

    Observe the design.

    What do you notice about the words, size of the lettering, arrangement, and overall design of the poster? Which words receive the most attention?

  2. 2

    Interpret the modern message.

    What message might a modern viewer take from the statement “If there is no struggle, there is no progress”? How does the poster’s design strengthen that message?

  3. 3

    Evaluate memory and context.

    What happens when one sentence from Frederick Douglass is removed from its original speech and presented to a modern audience by itself? What can this poster teach us about Douglass’s legacy, and what important historical context might be lost?
