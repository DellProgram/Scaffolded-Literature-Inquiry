Experiments on the Tight Rope

Douglass is no longer represented only as a fugitive or autobiographical narrator. In this 1852 political cartoon, he appears inside a national argument about abolition, party politics, and the presidential election.

Date
1852
Creator / Publisher
Edward Williams Clay / John Childs
Inquiry Use
Political perspective, abolition, and public influence

Source Context

Political cartoons argue through exaggeration.

The cartoon was published during the 1852 presidential election. It depicts Whig candidate Winfield Scott being pulled toward the presidency by several political figures, including Frederick Douglass, while weights marked “Free Soil” and “Abolition” contribute to the rope breaking.

How to Analyze It

Decode

Identify labels, symbols, dialogue, and exaggeration.

Viewpoint

Determine the cartoonist’s political message.

Compare

Ask how Douglass might describe his role differently.

Guiding Questions

Look past the joke to find the argument.

Use specific visual evidence. Remember that a political cartoon reflects a creator's viewpoint rather than an objective description of events.

  1. 1

    Identify the visual evidence.

    What people, objects, labels, and symbols do you notice in the cartoon? Which details seem most important to understanding its message?

  2. 2

    Interpret the cartoonist's message.

    What message is the cartoonist communicating about Frederick Douglass and the abolitionist movement? Use at least two details from the cartoon as evidence.

  3. 3

    Consider perspective.

    How does this cartoon show the challenges Douglass faced as a public abolitionist? How might Douglass himself have viewed his role differently from the way he is portrayed here?
