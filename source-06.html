Part III · What Does Freedom Really Mean?

Source 6 of 14

The Nation's Problem

More than two decades after slavery ended in Washington, D.C., Frederick Douglass used an anniversary of emancipation to reflect on both progress and unfinished work. This speech asks us to consider whether ending slavery was the same thing as achieving equality.

Guiding Questions

Measure progress against Douglass's expectations.

Use Douglass's language as evidence, then connect this speech to the earlier celebration of voting rights in Source 5.

  1. 1

    Interpret Douglass's view of progress.

    Douglass describes the struggle for equality as having both successes and failures. What does this suggest about how he viewed the progress made since slavery was abolished?

  2. 2

    Consider occasion and audience.

    Why might Douglass have used an anniversary celebrating the abolition of slavery to discuss problems that still remained? How might this choice have affected his audience?

  3. 3

    Compare celebration with criticism.

    Compare this speech with the Fifteenth Amendment image in Source 5. How does Douglass's message challenge or complicate the idea that freedom and equality had been fully achieved? Use evidence from both sources.
