The Fifteenth Amendment. Celebrated May 19th, 1870

This commemorative print celebrates a major expansion of political rights after the Civil War. It gives us a chance to ask what freedom meant once slavery had ended—and whether legal rights alone were enough to create full citizenship.

Inquiry Use

Source Context

A celebration can still be evidence.

The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited denying the right to vote on the basis of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. This print celebrates that constitutional change and the political participation of Black men after emancipation.

Government record: Read the official Fifteenth Amendment at the National Archives.

How to Analyze It

Observe

Identify symbols of voting, citizenship, and celebration.

Purpose

Remember that a commemorative image emphasizes achievement.

Compare

Use the official Fifteenth Amendment text to check what the law guaranteed.

Guiding Questions

Move from celebration to interpretation.

Use details from the print as evidence, then connect those details to the larger question of what freedom and citizenship meant after slavery.

  1. 1

    Identify signs of freedom and citizenship.

    What people, symbols, scenes, and objects do you notice in the image? Which details seem connected to freedom, citizenship, or political participation?

  2. 2

    Interpret the artist's message.

    What message does the artist communicate about the Fifteenth Amendment? Use at least two details from the image as evidence.

  3. 3

    Expand the meaning of freedom.

    After reading Section 1 of the official Fifteenth Amendment, how does this commemorative image expand the legal language into a broader picture of freedom and citizenship? How does that idea connect to Frederick Douglass's arguments?
