Part III · What Does Freedom Really Mean?

Source 8 of 14

On the Lessons of the Hour

Near the end of his life, Frederick Douglass used this 1894 address to confront racial injustice and to question whether emancipation had produced full equality. This source asks students to examine what Douglass believed freedom still required decades after slavery had been abolished.

Selected Passage

Begin with Douglass's stated purpose.

In the opening pages, Douglass explains why he believes another perspective is necessary in the national discussion about race and justice.

“I am here to speak for, and to defend ... a long-suffering people, and one just now subject to much misrepresentation and persecution.”

Use this opening statement as an entry point, then continue into the digitized speech to locate evidence for the guiding questions.

Guiding Questions

Use Douglass's argument to define unfinished freedom.

Identify his central concern, analyze his argument, and connect this late-life speech to evidence you have already examined.

  1. 1

    Identify the injustice.

    What problem or injustice does Douglass identify in this speech? What words or ideas help you determine what concerns him most?

  2. 2

    Analyze his argument about equality.

    What argument does Douglass make about freedom and equality? Choose one piece of evidence from the speech and explain how it supports his argument.

  3. 3

    Connect this speech to the inquiry.

    How does this speech show that Douglass's idea of freedom went beyond the abolition of slavery? Compare his message here with at least one earlier source we have examined.
