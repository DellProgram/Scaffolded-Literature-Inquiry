The Essential Question

Now that you have the historical context, you will now analyze a set of primary and secondary sources to decide what the evidence reveals about Frederick Douglass, freedom, citizenship, and historical memory.

Keep this question in mind as you work:

How did Frederick Douglass use his experiences, his words, and his public influence to challenge ideas about freedom and citizenship in the United States?

Think Like a Historian

Historians rarely rely on one source. They compare evidence, ask who created it, why it was created, and choose what it can—and cannot—tell them about the past. You will do the same throughout this inquiry.

Your final response should draw from the main book, the historical background, and the source set. The goal is not to collect facts about Frederick Douglass. The goal is to develop an evidence-based interpretation of how his experiences and public work challenged the nation's understanding of freedom and citizenship.

How it Works

Each document asks you to notice first, analyze second, and connect the evidence to the larger inquiry.

01

Examine

Look closely before making conclusions. Read the title, date, creator, labels, images, and other visible details.

02

Contextualize

Ask when the source was created, who created it, who the intended audience may have been, and what was happening at the time.

03

Interpret

Identify the source's message or perspective. Support your interpretation with specific evidence rather than general impressions.

04

Connect

Decide how the source adds to, complicates, or challenges ideas from the book and from sources you have already examined.

05

Evaluate

Consider what the source is useful for and what it cannot tell you. A source can be valuable without giving the whole story.

06

Collect Evidence

Save strong details, quotations, observations, and source numbers that you may want to use in your final argument.

Four Parts

The source order is intentional. Each part should change or deepen the way you think about the essential question.

Part I Sources 1–3

Experience and the Making of Frederick Douglass

Begin with place, personal experience, escape, and the formation of Douglass's public identity.

Keep asking: How did Douglass's experiences shape the ideas he later communicated to others?

Part II Sources 4–5

Douglass Becomes a Public Voice

Examine how Douglass, abolition, political rights, and public influence appeared in nineteenth-century visual culture.

Keep asking: How did other people represent Douglass and the causes associated with him?

Part III Sources 6–9

What Does Freedom Really Mean?

Use Douglass's later speeches, political writing, and a photograph to investigate citizenship, equality, political participation, and unfinished freedom.

Keep asking: What is the difference between ending slavery and achieving full citizenship?

Part IV Sources 10–14

Who Gets to Define a Legacy?

Compare memorial documents, newspapers, commemorative writing, biography, and a modern poster to see how Douglass has been remembered over time.

Keep asking: What changes when other people decide which parts of a person's life should be remembered?
